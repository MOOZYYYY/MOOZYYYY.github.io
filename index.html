SQL Sales Dashboard: From 540k raw invoice lines to management reporting

For the same UK online retailer, I designed a star-schema database, wrote the transformations and 13 analysis queries in SQL (cohort retention, growth, customer tiers, ABC analysis), cross-checked the results against an independent calculation, and built an interactive dashboard on top of them.

13SQL queries: CTEs, LAG, NTILE, running sums
60%of revenue from the top 10% of customers
22% → 80%of products make up 80% of revenue (ABC)
£0.00difference between SQL and the independent pandas check

What I did

  • Modelled the data as a star schema (fact table + customer, product and date dimensions) with keys, indexes and data-quality checks on every build
  • Wrote cohort retention, month-over-month growth with LAG(), customer deciles with NTILE() and an ABC product analysis in SQL
  • Caught real analytics traps: the AOV per-line vs per-order mistake, partial months, and a left-censored first cohort

Key insight

  • Repeat customers run the business: 88% of November revenue came from returning customers
  • The second order is the slow one: 35% of customers wait over 3 months before ordering again
  • Recommendation: account-manage the top 10%, follow up 6–8 weeks after a first order, and review the 53% of products that make only 5% of revenue

Demand Forecasting & Inventory Optimisation: How much should a retailer order each week?

For the same UK online retailer as my segmentation project, I forecast weekly demand for its 5 top products, measured how wrong the forecasts are, and fed that uncertainty into a mixed-integer programme that plans orders under warehouse space, budget and ordering costs. Then I simulated it on 12 weeks of real demand against simpler policies.

−32%inventory cost vs "order what you sold"
1 in 6units of demand lost when ordering exactly the forecast
39 vs 60orders placed in 12 weeks (optimiser vs rule of thumb)
~1,200warehouse units, beyond which extra space stops paying off

What I did

  • Built weekly demand from 540k+ invoice lines, adjusting for Saturdays, Christmas and bank holidays, and split off bulk wholesale orders (1% of lines, 12–31% of units) as unforecastable
  • Back-tested 6 forecasting models week by week; simple exponential smoothing won (29% error vs 37% for the naive forecast)
  • Formulated a MILP (SciPy/HiGHS) with expected lost sales from the forecast uncertainty, so the model chooses its own safety stock

Key insight

  • A good forecast isn't enough: you have to plan for its error
  • The optimiser beat every benchmark on test and validation weeks and at every warehouse size, mainly through fewer, batched orders
  • Recommendation: optimised ordering, bulk orders taken in advance, and warehouse space of up to ~1,200 units

Churn Prediction & Retention ROI: Who is worth a retention offer?

A telecom company loses 26.5% of its customers. Discounts cost money, and most go to customers who would have stayed anyway. I predicted each customer's churn risk, then put dollar values on every outcome to find the targeting rule that maximises campaign profit, and tested how that decision changes with the offer's economics.

−$12k → $98koffer to everyone vs model-based targeting
78%of churners reached by contacting 40% of customers
0.28profit-optimal threshold (not the default 0.5)
0.84ROC-AUC on unseen customers

What I did

  • Explored 7,043 customers: month-to-month contracts are 55% of customers but 89% of churners, and 53% leave in their first 6 months
  • Compared logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting with cross-validation, then chose the simplest model on purpose: equally accurate, explainable and well calibrated
  • Built a cost-benefit model and derived the break-even churn probability (~0.31), which the data-driven optimum (0.28) confirmed

Key insight

  • Offering a discount to everyone loses money; targeting the riskiest 40% turns the same idea into ~$98k profit
  • The right threshold depends on the offer: from targeting broadly to not running the campaign at all
  • Recommendation: A/B test the offer to measure its real success rate before scaling

Customer Segmentation: Who should a retailer protect, grow, or let go?

A UK online gift retailer sends the same marketing to every customer. I segmented 4,320 customers from 540k+ transactions with RFM analysis and K-means clustering, compared both methods, and turned the results into an action and budget priority for each group.

64%of revenue from the VIP cluster (16% of customers)
74%of revenue from the top 20% of customers
1 in 3customers ordered only once
0.996cluster stability (Adjusted Rand Index)

What I did

  • Cleaned 541,909 transactions, netting out £472k of cancellations instead of deleting them (one customer ordered 80,995 units and cancelled 12 minutes later)
  • Built RFM scores and 10 rule-based customer segments
  • Showed K-means fails on raw skewed data, then log-transformed and chose k = 4 using elbow, silhouette and business usefulness

Key insight

  • The two methods broadly agree, but K-means hid churn risk: 89% of high-value customers who had gone quiet were placed in an "ordinary" cluster
  • Recommendation: use K-means for strategy and RFM rules as churn alerts; focus on protecting VIPs and winning the second order from new customers
